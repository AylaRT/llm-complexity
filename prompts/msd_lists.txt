# Task
You annotate a short English text written by a beginning learner of English.
Following the guidelines below, identify five kinds of units and list them.
Do not calculate or score anything. Only list the units.

# Guidelines

Formulaic non-sentences are not annotated: Hi!, The end.

1. Word: a string of alphanumeric characters delimited by spaces or a space and a punctuation mark
  Examples:
  - I like my new school, class, teachers en friends: 9 words.
  Exceptions:
  - A wrongly joined or split word form is counted as the number of words in its correct form: eachother as two words, every body as one word.
  - A contraction is counted as the number of words in its expanded form: isn’t as two words, can’t as one word.
  - A proper noun is counted as one word: New Zealand as one word.
  - A possessive marker is not counted as a separate word: my friend’s as two words.
  - A placeholder standing in for redacted content (for example numb, used for a number) is counted as one word.


2. Sentence: a group of words delimited by sentence-final punctuation marks (period, question mark, exclamation mark, quotation mark, or ellipsis)
  Examples:
  - <sentence>I was skiing and it was very good snow.</sentence> <sentence>Better than ever!</sentence>
  Exceptions:
  - A run-on sentence is reanalyzed as two or more sentences, separated at the place of the missing conjunction or missing/inappropriate punctuation: <sentence>That was everything what I wanted to tell about XX</sentence> <sentence>I really want to recommend this holiday to you.</sentence>

3. T-unit: an independent clause together with its dependent clauses. A sentence fragment punctuated by the writer with no overt verb is also considered a T-unit.
  Examples:
  - <t-unit>My favorite subject is Music because I love making music</t-unit> and <t-unit>I also like Dutch</t-unit>.
  - <t-unit>Better than ever</t-unit>.
  Exceptions:
  - T-units in a run-on sentence are annotated in accordance with how the run-on sentence is reanalyzed as sentences: <t-unit>That was everything what I wanted to tell about XX</t-unit> <t-unit>I really want to recommend this holiday to you</t-unit>.


4. Clause: a structure with a subject and a finite verb. A sentence fragment punctuated by the writer with no overt verb is also considered a clause.
  Examples:
  - <clause>It would be a kind of present to my parents and sister to thank them</clause> for <clause>what they did for me</clause>.
  - <clause>Better than ever!</clause>.


5. Dependent clause: a finite adjective, adverbial, or nominal clause
  Examples:
  - It would be a kind of present to my parents and sister to thank them for <dependent-clause>what they did for me</dependent-clause>.


# Output format
Return exactly five blocks, in this order, each starting with the header below in capitals on its own line, then the requested output. No numbering, no bullets, no extra text, no blank lines inside a block. The headers and layout below are only about how to report your answer; what counts as each unit is governed entirely by the guidelines above. Output as follows:

WORDS:
All counted words, separated by commas, in the order they occur, copying each word exactly unless a word rule above changes its form. Do not reproduce the text's own punctuation.

SENTENCES:
One sentence per line, copied exactly from the text, applying the sentence guideline above.

T-UNITS:
One T-unit per line, copied exactly from the text, applying the T-unit guideline above.

CLAUSES:
One clause per line, copied exactly from the text, applying the clause guideline above.

DEPENDENT CLAUSES:
One dependent clause per line, copied exactly from the text, applying the dependent clause guideline above; if there are none, write "none".
